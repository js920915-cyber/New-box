Abbreviations:

VBT= Velocity based training

ACLR = Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction

The aim of this blogpost are to share clinical experience on how to
integrate velocity based training (VBT) to to guide exercise intensity,
make decisions from one session to the next, manage fatigue, and to
improve explosive performance after anterior cruciate ligament
reconstruction (ACLR).

“How much weight can I lift after my ACLR?”

Three weeks after ACLRwith an allograft, a patient asked me a question
that changed how we approached his training: “How much weight can I
lift?” Before his injury, he had been a competitive bodybuilder and an
experienced coach, squatting and deadlifting +250kg. Therefore, a pair
of 2x10 kg dumbbells would barely challenge him. We needed to find a
meaningful training stimulus with respect to the biological healing,
postoperative restrictions, or his knee general response to exercises.
we started his return to squat with sit to stand from a box equal to 90
degree knee flexion. Adding VBT device to the movement, we could
estimate how quickly he would stand up. With a graded exposure approach
we added a barbell in a front squat position. The VBT transdurer then
tracked the load we were able to apply from early in his rehab. Instead
of talking about an estimated percentage of 1RM, we had a conversation
around which velocity we would allowed in this early stage and with this
range of motion. To keep the motivation high we allowed less than 70% of
1RM. In this case he had to look for the speed per repetition rather
than a number of reps.

What is velocity-based training?

Velocity-based training (VBT) is a method of prescribing and monitoring
resistance training by measuring how fast a weight is lifted. The
principle is simple: as the weight increases, the lifting velocity
generally decreases. This relationship is known as the load–velocity
relationship [2,3].

By using a velocity measurement device, such as a linear position
transducer, we can measure lifting speed in meters per second (m/s).
This information helps us estimate the relative training intensity (% of
1RM).

Why use VBT during ACLR?

After ACLR, restoring quadriceps and hamstring strength is a major goal.
But finding the right load is not straightforward. Too little resistance
may fail to stimulate adaptation; too much may increase pain, swelling,
or knee irritability. We often prescribe strength work as a percentage
of one-repetition maximum (1RM), yet a patient’s capacity can change
with fatigue, sleep, recovery, and symptoms. In the earlier stages,
maximal testing may also be inappropriate. RIR (reps in reserve) is also
used, but patients new to daily training often struggle to judge it
accurately, which can push them harder than intended..

VBT adds to that conversation. If we know how fast someone moves a given
weight, we can estimate relative intensity and adjust the session to
their performance that day. The “velocity number” therefore helps the
patients to understand what a lighter, moderate, and heavier efforts
actually feel like. This is called autoregulation.

Imagine a patient who normally squats 60 kg at 0.80 m/s. On another day,
the same load moves at only 0.65 m/s. That does not automatically mean
that the patient is weaker, but it gives us a reason to check how the
patient feels, and whether the planned session still makes sense. Rather
than forcing the original load, we can adapt the training target.

Which velocity metrics do we look at?

To keep this easy to follow, we explain these metrics using the squat as
our reference exercise. The same ideas apply later to other lifts, but
the squat makes the phases of the movement clearer.

Three metrics are particularly relevant when applying VBT:

-   Peak velocity: This is the highest speed reached during the upward
    movement of the squat. It tells us how fast the barbell moved at its
    fastest point.

-   Mean velocity: This is the mean speed throughout the entire upward
    movement, from the bottom of the squat until we reach standing. It
    includes both acceleration, and the braking phase, just before
    standing position.

-   Mean propulsive velocity: This is the mean speed during the
    propulsive portion of the upward movement. Unlike concentric mean
    velocity, it excludes the final braking phase, when you slow the
    movement down, as you approach the standing position in a squat.
    This can be very useful in the intermediate phases of rehab to tell
    apart two athletes who look similar on mean velocity yet have
    different levels of strength.

Three uses of VBT in ACL rehab:

Assessment, monitoring and load adjustment

Over time, we found that velocity is useful not only for choosing the
load, but also for deciding what kind of session to deliver. A simple
reference squat can act as a daily “thermometer”: the same exercise, the
same load, and the same velocity metric, compared with previous
sessions.

In our clinical experience, VBT has three complementary purpose that
work together from one session to the next: Assessment, monitoring and
load adjustment

Assessment: We build an individual load–velocity profile, usually from
the squat, to understand how that patient moves across a range with
different sub-max loads. Fx 20kg, 30kg and 40kg. From that profile we
can estimate relative intensity and approximate 1RM without relying on
frequent maximal testing.

Monitoring: Each session we use a daily squat “thermometer”: the same
fixed reference load for 3×5 repetitions with maximal intended
concentric speed, tracking concentric mean velocity against that
patient’s recent sessions. This tells us how ready they are today before
we decide what the strength work should look like.

Load adjustment: Rather than locking the session to a fixed percentage
of 1RM, we prescribe by a target velocity within a set. Then we use a
velocity-loss threshold within the set to decide the numbers of
repetitions.

Assessment: Choosing a training load from lifting velocity

We can use lifting velocity to estimate relative training intensity
without repeatedly testing maximal strength. However, velocity is
exercise-specific. Because this article centers on the squat as our
reference exercise, the velocities we discuss are best read in that
context. Other exercises (for example a deadlift) can show different bar
speeds at the same percentage of 1RM. In this example, the velocity is
influenced by the weight on the barbell and the athlete body weight
whereas during the deadlift, the load are moved from the floor without
the bodyweight. Therefore, 70% of 1RM in a squat are performed at
0.77m/s, whereas in the deadlift 70% of 1RM are performed at 0.62m/s
(Table 1).

Speed zones: from moving fast to lifting heavy

To make the force–velocity relationship easier to understand, we
describe three broad zones: speed-strength, strength-speed, and absolute
strength. Light loads can generally be moved faster, while heavy loads
move more slowly. These zones help us explain the goal of a session, but
their percentages and velocities are approximate and differ between
exercises and individuals [1, 2].

Monitoring: Our daily test: a practical “thermometer”

In clinical practice, we introduce VBT once the patient demonstrates
good squat depth and with a movement quality. A typical session starts
with a warm-up before moving into three sets of five back squats. These
are performed with maximal intended concentric speed at a fixed
reference load. For example, 20, 30, and 40 kg barbell, depending on the
person. With VBT we then track concentric mean velocity, and compare it
with that patient’s recent sessions.

If the reference load moves at a similar or faster velocity than usual,
and the knee is responding well, we may continue with the planned
strength session. If it moves noticeably more slowly, we have two
options: change the session towards lower-demand exercises, such as
mobility,or reduce the load to match another intended training velocity.
We prefer to interpret the number alongside pain, swelling, movement
quality, and the patient’s injury history and lifting experience.

Load adjustment: Knowing when to stop: velocity loss within a set

Consider a squat set in which the first repetition is lifted at 0.80
m/s. Each repetition becomes a little slower. In this example, 0.64 m/s
represents a 20% velocity reduction from 0.80 m/s. A patient could stop
at that point even if more repetitions were originally planned or if
less reps were planned, he could have continued until he reached the 20%
drop in velocity.

In practice, this means we do not always have to insist on completing a
fixed number of repetitions. We can set a velocity-loss limit and stop
the set when performance drops beyond it.

The chosen velocity loss depends on the goal of the exercise. Lower

50% loss would allow 10 repetitions. We usually decribe 20% velocity
loss in our ACLR rehabilitation.

How we progress VBT across ACLR rehabilitation?

In our clinical practice, we gradually progress from lighter resistance
towards heavier strength work while introducing faster exercises as the
patient meets the relevant criteria. The time frames below illustrate
one approach; they are not automatic clearance dates or a validated ACLR
protocol.

-   0–3 months: Building strength and training tolerance

During approximately the first 3 months, we generally emphasize
controlled resistance training below 70% of estimated 1RM, with the aim
of rebuilding strength and loading tolerance. Surgical restrictions,
symptoms, and exercise selection take priority over any percentage
target.

-   3–6 months: Increasing strength and introducing strength-speed

Between approximately 3–6 months, we may introduce faster strength-speed
work. Again using the squat as our main reference exercise. We aim for a
mean concentric velocity of approximately 0.75–1.00 m/s, depending on
the training goal, while maintaining good movement quality. while
maintaining good movement quality. As the patient progresses, we may
introduce more explosive exercises,That velocity can also mark our entry
point for adding more load to the squat. At this stage we can start
building an initial force–velocity profile to map strength zones for
that individual. Mean propulsive velocity may be useful here, because —
as explained earlier — it tends to be more accurate with lighter loads.
As the patient progresses, we may introduce more explosive exercises.

-   From approximately 4.5 months: Developing explosive performance

From around 4.5 months, when the athlete meets the relevant strength and
movement criteria, we continue refining the force–velocity
(load–velocity) profile.

From around 4.5 months, when the athlete meets the relevant strength and
movement criteria, this is a good point to start thinking in a
force–velocity (load–velocity) profile for that patient, still with the
squat as our main reference. Mean concentric velocity can help us be
more accurate with the lighter loads we often use at this stage.
Velocity loss remains a key tool to manage fatigue in the set while we
keep progressing running, plyometrics, or change of direction. We also
keep using velocity in the warm-up as our daily readiness check or
¨thermometer¨ with the same fixed-load..

We may also introduce loaded squat jumps, for that, we prefer peak
velocity (it tends to be more accurate in the literature), though mean
propulsive velocity can also be used.

From around 4.5 months, we continue refining the load–velocity profile
to improve load prediction. We use velocity loss to managing fatigue
within a set. We may also introduce loaded squat jumps, using mean
propulsive velocity to monitor the propulsive portion of the movement
together with the peak velocity.

The daily readiness during the warm-up set may be using the thermometer
with the same fixed-load 3 × 5 reference sets described earlier.

-   After 6 months: Maximal strength and power

After 6 months, we often include heavier strength work at or above 85%
of 1RM alongside continued power training, provided the patient is ready
for it. We use velocity mainly for load prediction based on each
individual profile, and as a training tool for velocity loss and
feedback.

Clinical case example:

From rebuilding strength to producing force quickly

Later in rehabilitation we also want the athlete to produce force
rapidly when jumping. We may use velocity feedback during loaded squat
jumps to choose a load that still allows fast movement. If our clinical
goal is to improve concentric impulse in a countermovement jump (CMJ),
we can train with loaded jumps and keep velocity around 0.75–1.00 m/s.

Another example. We recently worked with a young tennis player who was
11 months post-ACLR. She had 90% limb symmetry during isokinetic
strength testing on the Biodex. One of our goals is to improve her
ability to generate force rapidly during the triple extension (hip, knee
and ankle) of a jump (also known a the propulsion impulse 2 (P2)
performed during a CMJ) and thereby contribute to better performance
during her tennis serve.

We target this with an explosive split squat progressing into a
single-leg calf raise. We positioned the safety bars to establish a
consistent starting position for the concentric phase, allowing her to
focus on accelerating the barbell upwards as quickly as possible. We
targeted a movement velocity to me between 1.00-0.75– m/s. VBT help us
reach the intended intensity in the exercise.

What we want readers to take away

For us, VBT creates the conversation around training intensity when the
patient is asking, “How much weight can I lift after ACL
reconstruction?”. Is the patient moving it as expected today? Are we
underloading or overloading, are repetitions slowing because fatigue is
accumulating, and is it time to emphasize heavier strength or faster
movement? What zone should be targeted at different exercises. The goal
with VBT is not simply to lift more weight, but deliver the target
training stimulus at the right time.
